# Business Cycle Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

Economies cycle through expansion, peak, contraction, and trough. The NBER officially dates US recessions using a holistic assessment of employment, income, spending, and production. Key leading indicators: yield curve inversion, ISM PMI below 50, rising jobless claims, declining consumer confidence, inverted real yield curve.

The Sahm Rule is a real-time recession indicator: when the 3-month average unemployment rate rises 0.5pp above its 12-month low, a recession has begun. It has a perfect track record since 1970.

Not all recessions are the same. Financial crisis recessions (2008) are deeper and longer than policy-induced recessions (1990, 2001). Pandemic recessions (2020) are sharp but can recover quickly if the financial system is intact.

## RIGOROUS

### NBER Framework
The NBER Business Cycle Dating Committee uses six indicators:
1. Real personal income less transfers
2. Nonfarm payroll employment
3. Real personal consumption expenditures
4. Wholesale-retail sales adjusted for price changes
5. Industrial production
6. Real GDP (quarterly)

A recession is "a significant decline in economic activity that is spread across the economy and lasts more than a few months." The committee weighs depth, diffusion, and duration — all three must be present.

Limitation: NBER dates recessions with significant lag (often 6-12 months after onset). By the time a recession is officially declared, it may already be over. Real-time indicators matter more for decision-making.

### Leading, Coincident, and Lagging Indicators
**Leading indicators** (turn BEFORE the economy):
- Yield curve slope (leads by 12-18 months)
- ISM Manufacturing PMI (leads by 2-6 months)
- Initial jobless claims (leads by 1-3 months)
- Building permits (leads by 6-12 months)
- Consumer expectations (University of Michigan, Conference Board)
- Stock prices (lead by 3-9 months, with many false positives)
- Conference Board Leading Economic Index (composite)

**Coincident indicators** (move WITH the economy):
- Nonfarm payrolls
- Industrial production
- Real personal income less transfers
- Manufacturing and trade sales

**Lagging indicators** (confirm AFTER the economy turns):
- Unemployment rate (lags by 3-6 months — peaks AFTER recession ends)
- CPI (lags by 6-12 months)
- Average duration of unemployment
- Bank lending rates (prime rate)
- Corporate profits

Critical implication: relying on lagging indicators for policy decisions guarantees being late. The unemployment rate is a lagging indicator — by the time it rises significantly, the recession is well underway.

### Sahm Rule (Detailed)
Sahm Recession Indicator = Current 3-month average unemployment rate - Minimum 3-month average unemployment rate over the prior 12 months.

- **Trigger threshold**: ≥ 0.50 percentage points
- **Track record**: Triggered in every recession since 1970, no false positives
- **Current value context**: Rising from low → 0.30 means we're 60% of the way to trigger. The rate of increase matters as much as the level.

Important nuance: the Sahm Rule identifies recession in real-time but does NOT predict recession in advance. By the time it triggers, the recession has already begun. Pre-Sahm warning signs:
- Sahm indicator rising above 0.20-0.30 (approaching threshold)
- Unemployment ticking up for 3+ consecutive months
- Payroll growth decelerating from 200K+ to below 100K

### Minsky's Financial Instability Hypothesis
Stability breeds instability. During prolonged expansions:
1. **Hedge finance** → borrowers can service debt from cash flows (healthy)
2. **Speculative finance** → borrowers can service interest but must refinance principal (vulnerable)
3. **Ponzi finance** → borrowers depend on asset price appreciation to service debt (fragile)

As the expansion matures, the composition shifts from hedge to speculative to Ponzi. Credit standards loosen, leverage increases, risk is underpriced. A small shock (rate increase, asset price decline) triggers cascading defaults. The "Minsky Moment" is the tipping point.

Indicators of Minsky buildup:
- Credit growth outpacing GDP growth
- Declining lending standards (Senior Loan Officer Survey)
- Compressed credit spreads (risk not being priced)
- Rising leverage ratios (corporate debt/EBITDA)
- Asset prices detaching from fundamentals (price/earnings, price/rent)

### Recession Typology
**Financial crisis recessions** (1990 S&L, 2008 GFC):
- Triggered by credit/banking sector collapse
- Deep and prolonged (average 18 months)
- Recovery is slow — balance sheet repair takes years
- Monetary policy is less effective (broken transmission mechanism)
- Characterized by: wide credit spreads, bank failures, credit contraction, asset price collapse

**Policy-induced recessions** (1980, 1981-82, 2001):
- Triggered by deliberate rate hikes to combat inflation
- Moderate depth (6-12 months typically)
- Recovery is relatively quick once policy reverses
- Characterized by: inverted yield curve, slowing investment, rising unemployment

**Exogenous shock recessions** (1973 oil crisis, 2020 COVID):
- Triggered by external event, not endogenous imbalances
- Depth depends on policy response speed
- Can recover quickly if financial system is healthy and policy response is aggressive
- Characterized by: supply disruption, rapid onset, sector concentration

### Cycle Positioning Indicators
To assess where we are in the cycle:
- **Early expansion**: Rising employment, improving confidence, loose financial conditions, steepening yield curve, credit spreads tightening
- **Mid expansion**: Strong job growth, rising wages, inflation stable, moderate credit growth, normal curve shape
- **Late cycle**: Tight labor market, rising inflation, flattening yield curve, elevated asset valuations, tightening financial conditions, credit growth outpacing GDP
- **Recession onset**: Rising claims, falling PMI below 50, inverted/uninverting curve, widening credit spreads, declining consumer spending
- **Trough/recovery**: Claims peaking, PMI bottoming and turning up, curve steepening, policy accommodation maximum
