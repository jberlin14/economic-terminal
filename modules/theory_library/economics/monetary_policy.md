# Monetary Policy Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

The Federal Reserve controls the federal funds rate to manage inflation and employment (dual mandate). Lower rates stimulate borrowing and spending; higher rates cool the economy. The key question is always: where is the "neutral rate" — the rate that neither stimulates nor restricts?

Policy transmission chain: Fed funds rate → short-term rates → yield curve → credit conditions → asset prices → real economy → employment → inflation → back to Fed.

Key signals: Real fed funds rate (nominal minus inflation) tells you if policy is actually restrictive or accommodative. A positive real rate above ~1.5% is restrictive. Near zero or negative is accommodative.

Forward guidance matters as much as actual rate moves. Markets price in expectations, so the Fed's communication about future policy can move markets before any action.

## RIGOROUS

### Taylor Rule Framework
The Taylor Rule provides a benchmark for where the fed funds rate "should" be:

r = r* + π + 0.5(π - π*) + 0.5(y - y*)

Where r* = neutral real rate (~0.5-1.0%), π = current inflation, π* = target (2%), y-y* = output gap.

When actual fed funds rate is BELOW the Taylor Rule prescription, policy is accommodative relative to conditions. When ABOVE, policy is restrictive. Persistent deviation in either direction signals eventual adjustment — either the Fed moves toward the rule, or the economy adjusts to force the Fed's hand.

### Transmission Mechanism (Detailed)
1. **Interest rate channel**: Fed funds → money market rates → bank lending rates → mortgage/corporate borrowing costs → investment & consumption decisions → aggregate demand
2. **Credit channel**: Higher rates tighten lending standards (financial accelerator). Banks reduce lending as collateral values fall. Small firms and leveraged borrowers are hit first.
3. **Asset price channel**: Higher rates reduce present value of future cash flows → equity valuations fall → wealth effect reduces consumption → portfolio rebalancing from risk assets to fixed income
4. **Exchange rate channel**: Higher US rates attract foreign capital → USD strengthens → exports become less competitive → trade deficit widens → net drag on GDP. Also: stronger dollar tightens financial conditions for USD-denominated EM debt.
5. **Expectations channel**: Forward guidance shapes inflation expectations. If the Fed credibly commits to 2% inflation, wage/price setting behavior adjusts accordingly. Loss of credibility is extremely costly to restore (Volcker required a deep recession).

### Lags and Non-Linearities
Monetary policy operates with "long and variable lags" (Friedman). Empirically:
- Financial conditions respond within weeks (market rates, asset prices)
- Real economy feels the impact in 6-18 months (investment, hiring)
- Inflation responds in 12-24 months (sticky prices, contract rigidity)

This creates the policy dilemma: by the time inflation data confirms policy is working, the economy may already be in recession from overtightening. The Fed must act on leading indicators, not lagging confirmation.

### Liquidity Trap / Zero Lower Bound
When rates hit zero, conventional policy is exhausted. Unconventional tools: QE (buying bonds to suppress long rates and inject reserves), forward guidance (committing to keep rates low for extended period), yield curve control (targeting specific long-term yields).

QE works primarily through: portfolio balance effect (pushing investors into riskier assets), signaling effect (demonstrating commitment to accommodation), and term premium compression (reducing the compensation investors demand for holding long bonds).

QT (quantitative tightening) is the reverse — Fed shrinks its balance sheet by letting bonds mature without reinvestment. This adds duration supply to the market, pushing term premium higher. The 2022-25 QT cycle removed ~$2T in Treasury/MBS holdings.

### Neutral Rate (r*)
The unobservable "neutral" or "natural" rate of interest — the rate consistent with full employment and stable inflation. Estimated via Laubach-Williams model or similar.

Pre-COVID estimates: ~0.5% real (2.5% nominal). Post-COVID: potentially higher due to fiscal expansion, structural deficits, deglobalization. If r* has risen and the Fed doesn't adjust, what appears "restrictive" may actually be closer to neutral.

Indicator: If the economy is growing at trend with stable inflation while the fed funds rate is at X%, then X% is approximately neutral. Persistent above-trend growth with rising inflation suggests policy is too loose; persistent below-trend growth with falling inflation suggests too tight.

### Policy Mistakes Framework
- **Type 1 (overtightening)**: Raising rates too far or keeping them high too long. Leads to unnecessary recession, unemployment overshoot. The Fed's historical bias — inflation-scarred institutions tend toward this error.
- **Type 2 (under-tightening)**: Not raising rates enough or cutting too early. Leads to inflation entrenchment, loss of credibility, eventual need for even more aggressive tightening. The 1970s Burns/Miller Fed.
- **Asymmetric risk**: In a high-debt economy, overtightening can trigger financial instability (bank failures, credit crunch) that is self-reinforcing. In a low-inflation-expectations environment, under-tightening has more room before expectations become unanchored.

### Incentive Structure
The Fed is institutionally biased toward fighting the last war. After 2008, the bias was toward accommodation (fear of deflation). After 2021-22, the bias shifted toward inflation-fighting (fear of credibility loss). FOMC members face career incentives: being wrong about inflation is more visible and punished than being wrong about unemployment. This asymmetry shapes policy even when the dual mandate is formally symmetric.
