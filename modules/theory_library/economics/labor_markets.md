# Labor Market Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

The labor market is a lagging indicator — unemployment rises AFTER the recession has begun and falls AFTER recovery is underway. But the rate of change is a leading signal: accelerating initial claims and decelerating payroll growth warn of trouble before the unemployment rate turns.

Key relationships: the Beveridge curve (job openings vs unemployment), the Phillips curve (unemployment vs inflation), and the participation rate (what share of working-age adults are in the labor force). Post-COVID, labor supply has been reshaped by early retirements, immigration patterns, and remote work — making historical comparisons less reliable.

The labor market is the Fed's second mandate and the last piece to break in a tightening cycle. The Fed needs to see labor market softening to be confident that inflationary pressure is fading.

## RIGOROUS

### Beveridge Curve
Plots the unemployment rate against the job vacancy rate. The position on the curve tells you about labor market tightness; shifts in the curve tell you about structural change.

- **Movement along the curve**: Normal cyclical dynamics. Recession pushes up and left (high unemployment, few openings). Expansion pushes down and right (low unemployment, many openings).
- **Outward shift**: Structural mismatch. The same vacancy rate produces higher unemployment because workers and jobs don't match (skills, geography, preferences). The post-COVID Beveridge curve shifted outward, suggesting increased mismatch.
- **Inward shift**: Improved matching efficiency. Technology, remote work, better labor market information.

Policy implication: if the curve has shifted out, the Fed may need higher unemployment to achieve the same inflation result. The "soft landing" scenario requires the curve to shift back inward — vacancies fall (normalizing labor demand) without unemployment rising sharply. This would mean firms reduce postings but don't fire workers.

### Okun's Law
The empirical relationship between output gap and unemployment gap:

u - u* = -0.5 * (Y - Y*) / Y*

A 1% decline in GDP below potential is associated with a 0.5pp rise in unemployment. This is relatively stable over time.

Implication: to bring unemployment up 1pp (say, from 3.5% to 4.5%), GDP would need to fall about 2% below potential — roughly consistent with a mild recession.

### Hysteresis
The hypothesis that cyclical downturns cause permanent structural damage to the labor market:
- Workers who are unemployed for long periods lose skills, networks, and motivation
- Firms reduce capacity during recessions and don't fully rebuild
- Young workers entering the labor market during recessions face permanently lower earnings trajectories

Evidence: the 2008-09 recession reduced labor force participation permanently. Workers who left during the downturn didn't return. The natural rate of unemployment may have risen.

Policy implication: there's a case for aggressive stimulus to prevent long-term unemployment from becoming permanent — the cost of a recession includes not just the immediate output loss but lasting structural damage. This argues against overtightening.

### Labor Market Indicators (Detailed)

**Employment Situation Report (monthly, first Friday)**
- **Nonfarm payrolls (establishment survey)**: Headline jobs number. MoM change is what matters, not the level. >200K = strong, 100-200K = moderate, <100K = weakening.
- **Unemployment rate (household survey)**: U-3 is the headline rate. U-6 includes discouraged workers and part-time for economic reasons — a broader measure of labor market slack.
- **Average hourly earnings**: Wage growth. 3.5%+ is consistent with above-target inflation (if productivity growth is ~1.5%). Below 3% suggests no wage-price spiral risk.
- **Average weekly hours**: A leading indicator within the report. Firms cut hours before cutting headcount. Declining hours = early warning of layoffs.
- **Labor force participation rate**: The denominator problem. Falling participation makes the unemployment rate look better than it is. The post-COVID decline reflected early retirements and disability claims.

**Jobless Claims (weekly)**
- **Initial claims**: The most timely labor market indicator. Levels below 250K historically consistent with expansion. Above 300K signals trouble. The trend matters more than any single week (volatile, seasonal adjustment noise).
- **Continuing claims**: How quickly the unemployed find new jobs. Rising continuing claims even as initial claims stabilize = hiring freeze (people aren't getting fired more, but they're not being rehired).

**JOLTS (monthly, significant lag)**
- **Job openings**: Demand for labor. The vacancies-to-unemployed ratio measures labor market tightness. Pre-COVID average: ~0.6. Post-COVID peak: 2.0. A ratio normalizing back toward 1.0 is consistent with a soft landing.
- **Quits rate**: A confidence indicator. Workers quit when they're confident they can find a better job. Falling quits rate signals worker anxiety about labor market conditions.

### Wage-Price Dynamics
The critical link between labor markets and inflation:
- Wage growth > productivity growth + inflation target = inflationary pressure
- Unit labor costs (wages per unit of output) are the key variable. If productivity is rising, wages can grow faster without inflationary pressure.
- The Employment Cost Index (ECI) is the best wage measure (avoids composition effects that distort average hourly earnings).

### Incentive Structure
- **Workers** have information asymmetry about their own productivity and outside options. In tight labor markets, their bargaining power increases, and the incentive to job-hop for wage gains is strong (the "great resignation" dynamic). In loose markets, the incentive is to accept current conditions.
- **Firms** hoard labor during mild downturns (it's expensive to hire and train replacements) but shed rapidly during sharp contractions. Labor hoarding creates a non-linearity: employment appears stable until it suddenly isn't. The transition from "hoarding" to "firing" is abrupt.
- **Government** faces political incentives around employment: visible job losses trigger voter backlash faster than inflation. This creates a bias toward accommodation and against preemptive tightening.
