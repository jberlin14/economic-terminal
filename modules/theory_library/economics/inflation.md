# Inflation Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

Inflation is a sustained increase in the general price level. Two primary drivers: demand-pull (too much money chasing too few goods) and cost-push (supply disruptions raising production costs). The Fed targets 2% PCE inflation — low enough for price stability, high enough to avoid deflation risk and give room for rate cuts.

Core inflation (excluding food and energy) is what the Fed watches for policy decisions because headline inflation is noisy. Within core, the key split is goods vs services — goods inflation can fall fast (supply chain normalization), but services inflation is sticky because it's driven by wages and shelter costs.

The Phillips Curve says there's a tradeoff between unemployment and inflation — tight labor markets push wages up, which pushes prices up. This relationship broke down from 2010-2019 (low unemployment, low inflation), leading many to declare it dead, but it reasserted itself in 2021-23.

## RIGOROUS

### Phillips Curve (Modern Version)
The New Keynesian Phillips Curve incorporates expectations:

π_t = β * E_t[π_{t+1}] + κ * (y_t - y*_t) + ε_t

Where π = inflation, β = discount factor (~1), κ = slope (sensitivity to output gap), ε = supply shocks.

Key insight: current inflation depends on EXPECTED future inflation, not just current slack. If expectations become unanchored (people expect high inflation to persist), actual inflation becomes self-fulfilling through wage demands and price-setting behavior.

The slope κ appears to be nonlinear — flat when inflation is low and stable (2010-2019), steep when inflation is high and salient (2021-2023). This means the Phillips Curve "wakes up" during inflationary episodes.

### NAIRU / Natural Rate
The Non-Accelerating Inflation Rate of Unemployment — the unemployment rate consistent with stable inflation. Not a fixed number; it shifts with:
- Demographics (aging workforce → lower participation → lower NAIRU)
- Labor market structure (gig economy, remote work, matching efficiency)
- Productivity growth (higher productivity → can sustain lower unemployment without inflation)

Pre-COVID NAIRU estimates: ~4.0-4.5%. Post-COVID: uncertain. If NAIRU fell (due to immigration, productivity), the labor market can be tighter without generating inflation. If NAIRU rose (due to labor market mismatch, early retirement), seemingly moderate unemployment rates mask inflationary pressure.

### Cost-Push vs Demand-Pull Decomposition
**Demand-pull indicators:**
- Broad-based price increases across categories
- Rising wages outpacing productivity
- Credit expansion, strong consumer spending
- Positive output gap (GDP above potential)
- Rising asset prices alongside rising consumer prices

**Cost-push indicators:**
- Concentrated in specific sectors (energy, food, supply-chain-sensitive goods)
- Wages lagging price increases (real wages falling)
- Supply disruptions identifiable (geopolitical, weather, pandemic)
- Output gap negative or closing (economy below potential)
- Producer prices rising faster than consumer prices (margin compression)

Policy implications diverge: demand-pull requires rate hikes to cool demand. Cost-push resolves on its own as supply normalizes — hiking into a supply shock risks unnecessary recession (the 1970s policy mistake was treating supply-driven oil inflation as demand-driven).

### Inflation Expectations (Critical)
The most important variable in inflation dynamics. Three measures:
1. **Market-based**: Breakeven inflation from TIPS spreads. Real-time, forward-looking, but contaminated by liquidity premia.
2. **Survey-based (professional)**: Survey of Professional Forecasters, Blue Chip. Stable, well-anchored, but backward-looking.
3. **Survey-based (household)**: University of Michigan, NY Fed. Volatile, influenced by gas prices, but important because households SET prices and wages.

Anchored expectations = inflation is self-correcting (shocks are temporary). Unanchored expectations = inflation becomes self-perpetuating (wage-price spiral). The entire post-Volcker monetary framework depends on expectations remaining anchored at ~2%.

### Wage-Price Spiral Dynamics
1. Prices rise → workers demand higher wages to maintain real income
2. Higher wages → higher production costs → firms raise prices
3. Higher prices → workers demand even higher wages → spiral

For this to sustain, three conditions must hold:
- Workers have bargaining power (tight labor market, unionization)
- Firms have pricing power (low competition, strong demand)
- Monetary policy accommodates (doesn't raise rates to break the cycle)

Break any link and the spiral ends. Volcker broke it by raising rates so aggressively that unemployment hit 10.8%, destroying worker bargaining power. Modern central banks aim to break it more gently through credible forward guidance.

### Shelter Inflation (The Structural Lag)
CPI shelter (owners' equivalent rent + rent of primary residence) is ~36% of core CPI. It lags market rents by 12-18 months due to measurement methodology (BLS surveys existing leases, which adjust slowly).

This means: when market rents peaked (mid-2022), CPI shelter didn't peak until late 2023. As market rents moderate, CPI shelter will mechanically decline — but slowly. Analysts who understand this lag can predict shelter CPI trajectory 12 months forward using current market rent data (Zillow, Apartment List).

### Fiscal Theory of the Price Level
An alternative framework: inflation is ultimately determined by fiscal sustainability, not just monetary policy. If government debt grows faster than the economy's ability to service it, and future surpluses are not credibly expected, the price level must adjust upward to make the real value of debt sustainable.

Implication: monetary policy alone cannot control inflation if fiscal policy is wildly expansionary. The central bank can raise rates, but if the government keeps running large deficits, eventually inflation absorbs the excess (fiscal dominance). The 2020-2022 experience — massive fiscal stimulus + accommodative monetary policy → highest inflation in 40 years — is consistent with this view.

### Incentive Structure
Inflation creates winners and losers, and those asymmetric incentives drive political pressure on policy:
- **Debtors benefit** from unexpected inflation (debt eroded in real terms) → governments with large debts face temptation to tolerate inflation
- **Creditors lose** from unexpected inflation → bond vigilantes demand higher yields, imposing market discipline
- **Workers with bargaining power** can protect real wages; those without (low-skill, non-union) bear the cost
- **Asset owners** are partially hedged (equities, real estate appreciate nominally); those without assets are fully exposed
- **Exporters benefit** from currency depreciation that accompanies inflation; importers and consumers lose

These incentives explain why inflation is politically contentious and why central bank independence matters — politicians face short-term incentives to tolerate inflation.
