# Sanctions and Economic Statecraft

## HIGH-LEVEL

Sanctions weaponize economic interdependence. They work by cutting targeted countries, entities, or individuals off from the global financial system, trade, or technology. The US has unique leverage because the dollar dominates global trade settlement and the US financial system is the hub of international capital flows.

Sanctions create market volatility through commodity supply disruptions (oil, gas, metals), FX dislocations (sanctioned currencies collapse), and credit risk repricing (counterparty exposure to sanctioned entities). They also accelerate dedollarization efforts and alternative payment system development.

The effectiveness of sanctions depends on: the target's vulnerability (import dependence, reserve adequacy, alternative partners), coalition breadth (unilateral vs. multilateral), and the sanctioning country's willingness to bear costs (energy price spikes, trade disruption).

## RIGOROUS

### Weaponized Interdependence (Farrell-Newman)
In a globally networked economy, certain nodes are disproportionately important. Control over these "chokepoints" provides coercive leverage:

1. **SWIFT messaging system**: Nearly all international bank transfers use SWIFT. Being cut off from SWIFT isolates a country financially. Used against Iran (2012, 2018) and Russia (2022).
2. **US dollar clearing**: International dollar transactions clear through US correspondent banks. OFAC sanctions can freeze any entity that touches the dollar system.
3. **Semiconductor supply chain**: TSMC + ASML + US chip design firms control advanced chip production. Export controls can deny a country access to advanced computing.
4. **CHIPS Act / technology controls**: Direct restrictions on technology transfer to strategic competitors.

The key insight: these chokepoints exist because of network effects and economies of scale, not coercion. Countries use them voluntarily because they're efficient. Sanctions exploit this voluntary dependence.

### Sanctions Effectiveness Framework
Sanctions "work" along multiple dimensions:
- **Coerce**: Force policy change in the target (rare — North Korea, Cuba, Iran haven't capitulated)
- **Constrain**: Limit the target's capabilities even if policy doesn't change (Russia's military-industrial capacity degraded by chip controls)
- **Signal**: Demonstrate resolve to allies and adversaries (sometimes the primary purpose)
- **Punish**: Impose costs as retribution (politically necessary even if economically questionable)

Empirical record (Hufbauer et al.): sanctions achieve stated policy objectives only ~30% of the time. Success correlates with:
- Modest objectives (human rights improvement easier than regime change)
- Strong economic leverage (target dependent on sanctioner)
- Multilateral coordination (reduces evasion routes)
- Target has limited alternative partners

### Market Transmission Channels
**Energy sanctions** (Russia, Iran, Venezuela):
- Supply reduction → oil/gas price spikes → inflation globally
- Supply redirection → Russian oil to China/India at discount → price bifurcation
- European energy crisis → EUR weakness, industrial recession, fiscal spending on energy subsidies
- Winners: alternative suppliers (US LNG, Gulf states), commodity traders

**Financial sanctions** (SWIFT removal, asset freezes):
- Target currency collapse (Russian ruble initially fell 50% in Feb 2022)
- Capital flight from sanctioned jurisdiction
- Counterparty risk repricing (banks reduce exposure to sanctioned regions)
- Secondary sanctions risk → overcompliance by global banks → trade finance disruption

**Technology sanctions** (semiconductor controls, entity lists):
- Long-term capability degradation in target country
- Supply chain disruption for firms dependent on sanctioned components
- Accelerated domestic development efforts (China's semiconductor self-sufficiency push)
- Revenue loss for sanctioning country's firms (lost sales to restricted market)

### Sanctions Evasion and Adaptation
Targets adapt over time, reducing sanction effectiveness:
- **Trade rerouting**: Using third countries (UAE, Turkey, Central Asia) as intermediaries
- **Alternative payment systems**: China's CIPS, Russia's SPFS as SWIFT alternatives (limited adoption so far)
- **Cryptocurrency**: Limited but growing role in evading financial sanctions
- **Barter and commodity-backed trade**: Russia-China energy-for-goods arrangements
- **Stockpiling**: Preemptive accumulation of sanctioned goods (chips, spare parts)

### Dedollarization Dynamic
Sanctions accelerate the search for dollar alternatives. The progression:
1. Bilateral trade settlement in local currencies (China-Russia, China-Saudi Arabia)
2. Alternative reserve assets (gold accumulation by central banks hit record in 2022-23)
3. Alternative payment messaging (CIPS adoption)
4. Alternative reserve currencies (CNY share of reserves rising from ~1% to ~3%)

This process is slow — the dollar's network effects are enormous. But each round of sanctions marginalizes the dollar in specific bilateral relationships, and the cumulative effect matters over decades. The dollar isn't losing reserve status, but its weaponization is creating a two-track system.

### Incentive Structure
- **Sanctioning government**: Faces domestic pressure to "do something" about adversary behavior. Sanctions are the tool between diplomacy and military force. The incentive is to impose sanctions visible enough for domestic audiences while avoiding costs to own economy. This leads to targeted sanctions on individuals (popular, low-cost) escalating to sectoral sanctions only when political pressure demands it.
- **Target government**: Must calculate whether capitulation or resistance is less costly. Authoritarian regimes can redirect costs to populations (who can't vote them out), making sanctions less effective against autocracies than democracies. The incentive is to signal resolve domestically while quietly seeking evasion routes.
- **Third countries**: Face secondary sanctions risk if they help the target evade. But also face commercial incentives to fill the trade gap (Chinese firms replacing Western suppliers in Russia). The incentive is to comply enough to avoid secondary sanctions while maximizing commercial opportunity.
- **Multinational firms**: Face reputational risk of staying in sanctioned markets AND financial risk of losing business. Most overcompliance by major firms (exiting Russia beyond what sanctions require) is driven by reputational incentives, not legal obligations.
- **Financial institutions**: Banks face catastrophic risk from sanctions violations (BNP Paribas: $9B fine). This creates extreme overcompliance — banks de-risk entire regions and populations, cutting off legitimate trade and remittances. The incentive structure produces humanitarian costs beyond what sanctions intend.
