# Fiscal Policy Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

Government spending and taxation are the fiscal levers. Deficit spending stimulates demand but crowds out private investment (higher rates from increased borrowing). The sustainability of deficits depends on the growth rate vs. the interest rate — if the economy grows faster than the interest rate on debt (g > r), deficits are sustainable. If not, debt spirals.

The US has run persistent deficits since 2001 with a brief exception. Debt/GDP rose from ~60% pre-COVID to ~120% post-COVID. The key question is whether markets will eventually demand higher term premium for holding US Treasuries (the "bond vigilante" scenario), or whether the dollar's reserve status provides indefinite fiscal space.

Fiscal policy directly affects inflation, growth, and interest rates — and therefore every market the terminal tracks.

## RIGOROUS

### Ricardian Equivalence (And Why It's Wrong in Practice)
The theoretical prediction that deficit-financed spending is equivalent to tax-financed spending because rational agents save the equivalent of future tax liabilities. In practice, this fails because:
- Households are liquidity constrained (can't borrow against future income)
- Households are myopic (don't plan 30 years ahead)
- Tax increases may fall on different people than current beneficiaries
- Political economy makes future tax increases uncertain (governments prefer to inflate away debt)

Implication: fiscal stimulus DOES boost aggregate demand, especially when targeted at liquidity-constrained households (stimulus checks, UI extensions, child tax credits). The multiplier varies by type of spending — transfer payments to low-income households have the highest multiplier (~1.5x); corporate tax cuts have the lowest (~0.3x).

### Debt Sustainability Framework
The government's intertemporal budget constraint:

Debt/GDP ratio is stable when: primary surplus/GDP ≥ (r - g) × debt/GDP

Where r = average interest rate on debt, g = nominal GDP growth rate.

- **If g > r**: The economy outgrows its debt. Deficits are "free" up to a point. This condition held from 2010-2021 (low rates, moderate growth).
- **If r > g**: Debt/GDP rises without primary surpluses. The government must either raise taxes, cut spending, inflate away the debt, or default. This condition is reasserting itself as rates normalize above growth.

Current US situation: ~120% debt/GDP, average interest rate on outstanding debt ~3.5% and rising (as low-rate bonds mature and are refinanced at higher rates), nominal GDP growth ~5%. The trajectory depends on whether interest costs continue rising faster than growth.

### Crowding Out
Higher government borrowing → increased supply of Treasuries → higher yields required to attract buyers → higher borrowing costs for private sector → reduced private investment.

The degree of crowding out depends on:
- **Economic slack**: If the economy is below potential (recession), crowding out is minimal — fiscal spending fills the demand gap. If at or above potential (overheating), crowding out is significant.
- **Federal Reserve response**: If the Fed accommodates fiscal expansion (keeping rates low), crowding out is delayed but inflation results. If the Fed tightens in response, crowding out is immediate.
- **Foreign demand**: If foreigners buy Treasuries (reserve accumulation, safe haven), fiscal deficits can expand without raising domestic rates. This is the "exorbitant privilege."

### Fiscal Dominance
The nightmare scenario for central banks: fiscal deficits so large that the central bank cannot tighten without causing a fiscal crisis (debt service costs explode). The central bank is forced to keep rates low and tolerate inflation to ensure the government can service its debt.

Indicators of approaching fiscal dominance:
- Interest payments exceeding defense spending (US crossed this threshold ~2024)
- Term premium rising (investors demanding compensation for fiscal risk)
- Growing political pressure to suppress rates or monetize debt
- Fiscal deficit expanding even during expansion (pro-cyclical fiscal policy)

### Modern Monetary Theory (MMT) — And Its Limits
The claim: sovereign currency issuers can always fund spending by creating money; the constraint is inflation, not solvency. Taxes are tools for managing aggregate demand and incentives, not for "funding" government.

What's correct: the US government, as issuer of the world's reserve currency, faces a much softer budget constraint than most countries. Default is a political choice, not an economic necessity.

What's wrong or risky: unbounded deficit spending DOES generate inflation when the economy is at capacity. The 2020-2022 experience demonstrated this. The constraint is real — it's just inflation rather than solvency. And once inflation expectations become unanchored, the cost of re-anchoring (Volcker recession) is enormous.

### Incentive Structure
- **Politicians**: Face electoral cycles (2-4 years). Incentive is to spend now and defer costs. Tax cuts are popular; spending cuts are not. The deficit is an abstraction to voters until it becomes a crisis. This creates a structural deficit bias — democracies systematically overspend relative to optimal policy.
- **Treasury Department**: Must finance the deficit. Faces a choice between short-duration (cheaper now, rollover risk) and long-duration (more expensive now, locked-in rate). Shifting toward shorter duration signals confidence OR desperation — context-dependent.
- **Bond market ("vigilantes")**: Investors discipline fiscal excess by demanding higher yields. But this mechanism only works when investors have alternatives (credible alternative reserve assets). The dollar's dominance means discipline is slow to arrive.
- **Rating agencies**: Face incentive to maintain stable ratings (clients are governments and issuers). Downgrades lag fiscal deterioration. When downgrades happen (S&P 2011, Fitch 2023), the market impact is often paradoxically small (where else does capital go?).
- **Future generations**: Bear the cost of current deficits but have no political voice. This is the fundamental political economy failure of fiscal policy — the costs are diffuse and deferred, the benefits are concentrated and immediate.
