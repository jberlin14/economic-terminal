# Trade Policy Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

Tariffs are a tax on imports. They raise prices for consumers and protected industries' competitors, generate government revenue, and provoke retaliation. Standard trade theory says tariffs reduce total welfare — but they redistribute income from consumers (who pay more) to producers (who face less competition) and government (tariff revenue).

The modern trade policy debate isn't about free trade vs. protectionism — it's about which industries and workers bear the adjustment costs of globalization, and whether governments can use tariffs strategically to reshape supply chains and geopolitical alignment.

Tariffs are inflationary (higher import prices pass through to CPI), growth-reducing (supply chain disruption, reduced specialization gains), and politically popular (concentrated benefits to protected industries, diffuse costs to consumers).

## RIGOROUS

### Optimal Tariff Theory
A large country (one that can affect world prices) can impose a tariff that improves its terms of trade — the foreign exporter absorbs some of the tariff cost by cutting prices. The "optimal tariff" maximizes the terms-of-trade gain minus the deadweight efficiency loss.

However, this only works if trading partners don't retaliate. In practice, tariffs trigger retaliatory tariffs, leading to a prisoners' dilemma: both countries end up worse off than under free trade, but neither can unilaterally disarm without being exploited.

The WTO/GATT framework exists precisely to solve this coordination problem — mutual tariff reduction where both sides benefit. When countries defect from this framework (unilateral tariffs, bilateral deals), the cooperative equilibrium breaks down.

### Stolper-Samuelson Theorem
Trade liberalization benefits the factor of production that the country has in abundance, and hurts the scarce factor.

For the US (abundant in capital, skilled labor; scarce in unskilled labor):
- Free trade benefits capital owners and skilled workers (can export high-value goods/services)
- Free trade hurts unskilled workers (competing with cheaper foreign labor)

This is the theoretical foundation for the "China shock" — US manufacturing employment declined as trade with China expanded. The aggregate US economy grew, but the gains were concentrated among capital owners and skilled workers while manufacturing communities bore the costs.

Tariffs reverse this: they protect unskilled workers in import-competing industries at the cost of higher prices for everyone and reduced exports for competitive industries.

### Strategic Trade Policy (Brander-Spencer)
In industries with high barriers to entry, large scale economies, and excess profits (e.g., aerospace, semiconductors, AI), government subsidies or protection can shift rents from foreign to domestic firms. The argument for industrial policy in "strategic" sectors.

Conditions for success:
- The industry must have genuine scale economies and supernormal profits
- Government must be able to identify winning industries (information problem)
- Trading partners must not retaliate effectively
- The subsidy must not be captured by rent-seeking insiders

In practice, industrial policy has a mixed track record. Successes (East Asian semiconductor development, US DARPA) coexist with expensive failures (Solyndra, Concorde). The incentive structure matters: are bureaucrats allocating capital based on strategic analysis or political connections?

### Gravity Model of Trade
Bilateral trade flows are proportional to economic size and inversely proportional to "distance" (geographic, cultural, institutional). This empirical regularity explains ~70% of trade patterns.

Implication: trade policy disruptions that increase "distance" (tariffs, sanctions, regulatory divergence) reduce trade volumes predictably. The gravity model can estimate the GDP cost of decoupling from specific trade partners.

### Trade Policy Transmission to Markets
**First-order effects (immediate):**
- Tariff announcement → affected sector stocks fall (higher input costs), protected sector stocks rise
- Currency of tariffed country weakens (expected lower exports)
- Bond markets price in lower growth (risk-off) and higher inflation (cost-push)

**Second-order effects (weeks/months):**
- Supply chain reorganization costs → margin compression across affected industries
- Retaliatory tariffs → US export sector damage
- Consumer price increases → CPI impact (typically 0.1-0.3pp per 10% tariff on broad imports)
- Business investment uncertainty → capex delayed → growth slowing

**Third-order effects (quarters/years):**
- Supply chain relocation (friend-shoring, nearshoring) → investment in new capacity
- Trade diversion (buying from non-tariffed countries, often at higher cost)
- Permanent efficiency loss from reduced specialization
- Geopolitical realignment of trade blocs

### Tariff Pass-Through Estimates
Empirical research on recent US tariffs (2018-2025):
- Import prices rose nearly 1:1 with tariff rates (foreign exporters did NOT absorb costs — contradicting optimal tariff theory for US-China trade)
- Retail price pass-through: ~30-60% of the tariff reaches consumers (retailers and importers absorb some via margin compression)
- Timeline: import prices adjust within 1-2 months; retail prices adjust within 3-6 months
- Inflation impact: 25% tariff on all Chinese imports → approximately 0.3-0.5pp one-time CPI increase

### Incentive Structure
- **Protected industries**: Strong incentive to lobby for tariffs (concentrated benefit). Steel, aluminum, autos, agriculture are perennial lobbying forces. The benefit per firm is large; the cost per consumer is small. This asymmetry drives protectionist policy even when aggregate welfare declines.
- **Exporting industries**: Incentive to oppose tariffs (retaliation hits them). But export industries often have less political salience than import-competing industries (soybean farmers vs. steel workers in electoral politics).
- **Consumers**: Bear the cost of tariffs but face collective action problem — no individual consumer lobbies against a 10% price increase on washing machines. Consumer interest is diffuse; producer interest is concentrated.
- **Trading partners**: Retaliation is driven by domestic political incentives — they must be seen responding to protect their own industries. This makes trade wars self-escalating: each retaliation triggers domestic political pressure for further escalation.
- **Politicians**: Tariffs are electorally useful — they provide visible protection to specific constituencies (steel workers in swing states) while the costs (higher prices) are invisible to most voters. The anti-trade political coalition now spans both parties in the US.
- **Multinational firms**: Face incentive to lobby against tariffs publicly while quietly restructuring supply chains. The net effect is often to shift production to third countries (Vietnam, Mexico) rather than reshore to the US — frustrating the stated policy objective.
