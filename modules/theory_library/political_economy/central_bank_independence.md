# Central Bank Independence Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

Central bank independence means the Fed sets interest rates without direct political control. This matters because fighting inflation requires short-term pain (higher unemployment) that politicians want to avoid. Independent central banks deliver lower inflation because they can credibly commit to price stability without being overruled by electoral incentives.

The key concept is "time inconsistency": before an election, the optimal promise is low inflation; after the election, the temptation is to stimulate growth (which eventually causes inflation). If markets know the central bank can be pressured, inflation expectations rise preemptively, making the actual inflation outcome worse.

Forward guidance is the modern extension — the Fed doesn't just set rates, it shapes expectations about FUTURE rates. Credibility is the asset; once lost, it takes a deep recession (Volcker 1981-82) to rebuild.

## RIGOROUS

### Time Inconsistency Problem (Kydland-Prescott, Barro-Gordon)
The fundamental problem: policymakers have an incentive to deviate from announced plans after private agents have set wages and prices based on those plans.

1. Central bank announces 2% inflation target → workers and firms set wages/prices accordingly
2. Once wages are locked in, the central bank faces temptation to create a "surprise" inflation burst → reduces real wages → stimulates employment → short-term growth boost
3. But rational agents ANTICIPATE this temptation → set wages higher to compensate → result is higher inflation with NO employment gain (inflation bias)

Solution: **commitment technology** — make it costly or impossible for the central bank to deviate from its target. Mechanisms:
- Legal independence from political interference
- Inflation targeting frameworks with public accountability
- Reputation and institutional culture (the Fed's "credibility capital")
- Appointing "conservative" central bankers who weight inflation more heavily than employment (Rogoff's argument)

### Credibility and Expectations
Central bank credibility is a stock variable — accumulated over years of consistent behavior, depleted quickly by policy errors or perceived political capture.

**High credibility regime** (post-Volcker through 2019):
- Inflation expectations anchored at 2%
- Supply shocks (oil prices, etc.) cause temporary inflation spikes that self-correct
- The Fed can cut rates aggressively in recessions without fears of inflation
- Policy is "automatically stabilizing" because expectations do the work

**Low credibility regime** (1970s, and the risk of a future re-emergence):
- Inflation expectations unanchored
- Supply shocks trigger persistent inflation (second-round effects through wages)
- The Fed must overtighten to prove commitment, causing deeper recessions
- Every policy action faces skepticism

The transition between regimes is asymmetric: losing credibility is fast (a few policy mistakes or appearances of political pressure); rebuilding is slow and painful (requires demonstrated willingness to cause recession).

### Forward Guidance Framework
Modern monetary policy operates through three channels:
1. **Current rate setting**: Traditional tool, affects short-term rates directly
2. **Forward guidance**: Shaping expectations about future rate path. "Rates will stay low for an extended period" or "We will raise rates until inflation reaches 2%"
3. **Balance sheet policy**: QE/QT affects long-term rates and portfolio allocation

Forward guidance types:
- **Calendar-based**: "Rates will stay near zero through 2024" — clear but inflexible
- **Outcome-based**: "Rates will stay low until unemployment falls below 5%" — flexible but requires markets to believe the central bank's reaction function
- **Delphic** (forecast): "We expect rates to remain low" — no commitment, merely a forecast
- **Odyssean** (commitment): "We WILL keep rates low regardless of inflation developments" — strong commitment, high credibility cost if violated

### Political Pressure on Central Banks
Threats to independence and their market implications:

**Explicit pressure**: Politicians publicly demanding rate cuts or firing central bank officials. Market impact: immediate rise in long-term yields and inflation expectations as credibility is questioned.

**Implicit pressure**: Appointing politically loyal governors, tying central bank budget to government approval, expanding the mandate to include objectives that conflict with price stability (e.g., climate, inequality).

**Fiscal dominance** (covered in fiscal_policy.md): When fiscal deficits are so large that tightening monetary policy would trigger a fiscal crisis. The central bank is implicitly forced to accommodate.

**Populist pressure**: Both left-populism ("keep rates low for working people") and right-populism ("the Fed is sabotaging the economy") threaten independence. The common thread is prioritizing short-term political objectives over long-term price stability.

### The Fed's Reaction Function
The implicit "rule" the Fed follows in setting rates. Not a mechanical formula (they explicitly reject being bound by rules), but an observable pattern:

Currently observed priorities:
1. Inflation returning to 2% (primary, post-2021 trauma)
2. Financial stability (liquidity crisis prevention)
3. Employment not deteriorating sharply
4. Communication consistency (avoiding market disruption)

The order of priorities shifts with the regime:
- Post-2008: Employment first (inflation below target, no urgency)
- Post-2021: Inflation first (above target, credibility at stake)
- Crisis mode: Financial stability first (everything else secondary to preventing systemic collapse)

### Incentive Structure
- **Fed Chair**: Appointed for 4-year term, renewable. Faces dual incentives — maintain institutional credibility AND avoid being blamed for recession. The political party of the appointing president creates implicit pressure (but historically, Chairs have maintained independence across administrations).
- **FOMC members**: Regional Fed presidents rotate voting seats. They have reputational incentives — being consistently wrong or consistently in the minority damages influence. Regional bank presidents tend toward hawkishness (close to Main Street banks, less sensitive to Wall Street); Board governors toward dovishness (appointed by president, more responsive to political currents).
- **Market participants**: WANT Fed independence because it anchors inflation expectations and reduces uncertainty. But also want dovish policy (low rates = higher asset prices). This creates cognitive dissonance: markets punish perceived political interference but also celebrate rate cuts.
- **Politicians**: Short-term incentive to pressure the Fed for accommodation. Long-term cost of undermining independence is borne by future administrations. This temporal mismatch explains why independence is always under threat but usually survives — the long-term costs eventually materialize and reinforce the case for independence.
