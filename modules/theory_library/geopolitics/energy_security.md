# Energy Security and Commodity Geopolitics

## HIGH-LEVEL

Energy is the transmission mechanism between geopolitics and inflation. Oil price shocks are the most common channel through which geopolitical events hit the real economy and monetary policy. The US became a net energy exporter (shale revolution), which changes the dynamic — oil price spikes are less economically damaging but still inflationary.

OPEC+ controls ~40% of global oil production and uses supply management as a geopolitical tool. Russia's energy relationship with Europe (and its disruption post-2022) demonstrated how energy dependence creates political vulnerability. The energy transition adds a new layer — clean energy supply chains create new dependencies (Chinese dominance of solar panels, batteries, rare earth processing).

## RIGOROUS

### Resource Curse / Dutch Disease
Countries with abundant natural resources tend to have slower growth, weaker institutions, and more authoritarian governance than resource-poor peers. Mechanisms:

1. **Dutch Disease**: Resource exports strengthen the currency → manufacturing becomes uncompetitive → economy becomes dependent on single commodity → vulnerability to price collapse
2. **Institutional degradation**: Resource rents allow governments to buy loyalty without taxation. Without taxation, citizens have less leverage to demand accountability. The social contract atrophies.
3. **Volatility**: Commodity prices are highly volatile → fiscal revenue swings wildly → boom-bust cycles → pro-cyclical policy (spend during booms, cut during busts — exactly backwards)

Market relevance: resource-dependent EM currencies (RUB, NGN, ARS, VEF) are proxies for commodity prices. Their central banks face impossible choices — raise rates to defend currency (choking growth) or cut rates to support growth (accelerating depreciation).

### Petrodollar System
Oil priced and traded in US dollars creates structural demand for dollars globally. Oil importers must hold dollar reserves; oil exporters invest surplus dollars in US Treasuries and financial assets (petrodollar recycling).

Challenges to the system:
- China buying oil from Saudi Arabia/Russia with yuan
- Russia redirecting energy trade to yuan/rupee settlement
- Digital currencies potentially bypassing dollar intermediation

Current reality: the petrodollar system is eroding at the margins but remains dominant. Oil markets are too liquid and too dollar-denominated for rapid transition. The key metric to watch is the share of oil trade settled in non-dollar currencies (rising from ~5% to ~15-20%).

### OPEC+ Dynamics
OPEC+ (OPEC + Russia and other non-OPEC producers) manages supply to target a price range. The cartel faces classic collective action problems:

- **Cheating incentive**: Each member benefits from producing above quota while others cut (prisoner's dilemma). Saudi Arabia as the "swing producer" bears disproportionate adjustment costs.
- **Price target asymmetry**: High-population producers (Saudi Arabia, Iran, Iraq, Nigeria) need high prices for fiscal breakeven ($70-90/bbl). Low-population producers (UAE, Kuwait) can tolerate lower prices. This creates internal tensions.
- **Russia's role**: Russia joined OPEC+ for geopolitical leverage. Its compliance with cuts is unreliable (strong incentive to maximize revenue under sanctions). US sanctions on Russian oil further complicate OPEC+ coordination.

Market regime mapping:
- OPEC+ cutting aggressively → oil prices rising → inflationary → Fed hawkish
- OPEC+ flooding market → oil prices falling → disinflationary → EM producer stress
- OPEC+ fragmentation → high volatility → uncertainty premium across commodities

### Energy Transition as Geopolitical Shift
The transition from fossil fuels to clean energy reshuffles geopolitical dependencies:

**Old dependencies** (declining but still dominant):
- Middle East oil → global energy security
- Russian gas → European heating and industry
- OPEC+ → global inflation dynamics

**New dependencies** (rising):
- Chinese control of solar panel manufacturing (~80% global capacity)
- Chinese dominance in battery production and processing (~75% lithium refining, ~85% cobalt processing)
- DRC (cobalt), Australia/Chile/Argentina (lithium), China/Myanmar (rare earths) — new "resource powers"
- Copper → electrification bottleneck (EVs, grid expansion)

The "green transition" doesn't eliminate energy security risk — it transforms it. Countries that trade oil dependence for battery metal dependence merely shift the vulnerability.

### Incentive Structure
- **Oil producers (governments)**: Need high prices for fiscal stability and regime survival (Saudi Arabia: ~$80/bbl fiscal breakeven). But also need to maintain market share against competitors and energy transition. This creates a Goldilocks pricing strategy — high enough for revenue, low enough to slow transition and discourage alternatives.
- **Oil consumers (governments)**: Want low prices for economic growth and political stability (high gas prices lose elections). But also want energy transition investment (requires high fossil fuel prices to make renewables competitive). This contradiction is resolved by subsidizing renewables while pressuring OPEC for supply — having it both ways.
- **Energy companies**: Face stranded asset risk from transition but current fossil fuel profits are enormous. The incentive is to extract maximum value from existing assets while making enough green investments for ESG/regulatory compliance. Long-cycle investment (deepwater, LNG) is declining, which constrains future supply.
- **China**: Controls clean energy supply chains by design (industrial policy + massive subsidies). The incentive is to maintain this dominance as leverage in great power competition. A country that controls the world's solar panels, batteries, and rare earth processing has a chokepoint equivalent to Middle Eastern oil.
- **Europe**: Most vulnerable to energy disruption (demonstrated 2022). The incentive is to diversify supply (US LNG, renewables, nuclear) and reduce dependence on any single source. This drives massive energy investment — fiscal implications (debt), industrial implications (competitiveness), and trade implications (US LNG relationship).
