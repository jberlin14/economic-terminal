# Great Power Competition Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

The US-China rivalry is the defining geopolitical dynamic for markets. It drives trade policy (tariffs, export controls), technology competition (semiconductors, AI), supply chain restructuring (decoupling, friend-shoring), and capital flow patterns (investment restrictions). Secondary effects include: European strategic autonomy push, EM countries hedging between blocs, and commodity supply chain vulnerability.

The "Thucydides trap" — the historical pattern where a rising power challenges an established power, often leading to conflict — frames the US-China dynamic. But the nuclear deterrent and economic interdependence make outright military conflict between the US and China less likely than the historical analog suggests. The competition is primarily economic, technological, and institutional.

Markets price geopolitical risk through: defense stocks, commodity risk premia (oil, rare earths, chips), FX (safe-haven flows), and credit spreads (EM exposure to conflict zones).

## RIGOROUS

### Hegemonic Stability Theory
The international economic order requires a hegemon willing to provide public goods: reserve currency, open markets, security guarantees, institutional frameworks (IMF, WTO, World Bank). The US has played this role since 1945.

As the hegemon's relative power declines, the incentive to provide these public goods weakens:
- **Free-rider problem intensifies**: Other countries benefit from US-provided stability without contributing proportionally (NATO burden-sharing debate)
- **Domestic backlash**: Hegemon's population questions the costs of maintaining global order (US foreign policy isolationism trends)
- **Challenger emergence**: Rising power (China) builds parallel institutions (AIIB, BRI, BRICS+) that fragment the order

Market implication: the erosion of hegemonic stability introduces structural uncertainty — higher risk premia across all markets, more frequent geopolitical shocks, less effective international coordination during crises. The 2008 coordinated global response was possible because of US hegemonic consensus; the next global crisis may lack such coordination.

### Thucydides Trap Framework
Allison's analysis of 16 historical cases where a rising power challenged a ruling power: 12 of 16 resulted in war.

US-China dynamics that fit the pattern:
- Economic weight approaching parity (PPP GDP roughly equal)
- Technological competition in strategic sectors (AI, quantum, biotech)
- Territorial disputes (Taiwan, South China Sea)
- Ideological competition (liberal democracy vs. authoritarian state capitalism)
- Alliance competition (US hub-and-spoke vs. China's BRI/SCO)

Mitigating factors:
- Nuclear deterrence (MAD prevents direct conflict)
- Economic interdependence (mutual cost of decoupling, though declining)
- Institutional frameworks (UN, WTO — weakened but still functional)
- No direct territorial dispute between US and China (Taiwan is indirect)

### Offshore Balancing vs. Deep Engagement
Two competing US grand strategy frameworks:

**Deep engagement** (current, bipartisan consensus):
- US maintains forward military presence (Europe, Asia, Middle East)
- Alliance network provides deterrence and influence
- Economic openness (with exceptions for strategic sectors)
- Cost: military spending, entanglement risk, overextension

**Offshore balancing** (academic alternative, gaining political traction):
- US reduces forward presence, relies on regional powers for local balancing
- Intervenes only when a potential hegemon threatens to dominate a key region
- Cost: alliance credibility loss, vacuum filling by adversaries, reduced influence

Market implications: a shift toward offshore balancing would mean reduced defense spending (negative for defense stocks), lower US engagement guarantee (higher regional risk premia), and potentially weaker dollar (reduced safe-haven demand).

### Technology Competition as Geopolitical Battleground
The US-China tech competition has replaced trade as the primary arena:

**Semiconductors**: The "new oil" of geopolitics. Advanced chip fabrication concentrated in Taiwan (TSMC) and Netherlands (ASML). US export controls aim to freeze China at ~2019 technology levels. China investing massively in domestic capability but 5-10 years behind on leading edge.

**AI**: Compute (chips), data (censorship vs. openness tradeoff), and talent (US advantage in top research). AI capabilities have military and economic implications that make them a national security priority.

**Market transmission**: Tech sector valuations partially reflect geopolitical positioning. Export control announcements → semiconductor stock moves. AI investment → power demand → energy market effects. Data regulation divergence → compliance costs for multinationals.

### Alliance Dynamics and Market Risk
**NATO/Europe**: US security guarantee enables European economic model (low defense spending, high social spending). Questioning this guarantee (burden-sharing debate) → European defense spending increases → fiscal implications → bond market effects.

**Indo-Pacific**: AUKUS, Quad, bilateral treaties with Japan/Korea/Philippines. China's military buildup → regional arms race → defense sector growth → commodity demand (metals, energy).

**Middle East**: US pivot away from the region → reduced security umbrella → Saudi Arabia/UAE hedging toward China → oil market implications (pricing in non-dollar currencies, OPEC+ dynamics).

### Incentive Structure
- **US political establishment**: Bipartisan consensus on China competition (rare in polarized politics). The incentive is to appear "tough on China" regardless of party. This creates a ratchet effect — each administration imposes more restrictions, and relaxing them is politically costly.
- **China's leadership**: Faces legitimacy based on economic growth and national rejuvenation. The incentive is to develop self-sufficiency (reduce vulnerability to US sanctions/controls) while avoiding confrontation that disrupts growth. Taiwan is the exception — perceived as a core interest where domestic political costs of inaction exceed costs of escalation.
- **US allies**: Face the "dual hedging" dilemma — security dependence on the US, economic dependence on China. Japan, Korea, Australia, and Europe all want to avoid being forced to choose. The incentive is to maintain strategic ambiguity as long as possible, which becomes untenable if US-China tensions escalate to conflict.
- **Multinational corporations**: Facing "decoupling" pressure from both sides. The incentive is to build redundant supply chains ("China plus one") — costly but necessary for risk management. This drives the nearshoring/friend-shoring investment theme.
- **Emerging markets**: Can play both sides, extracting concessions from US and China (India, Indonesia, Vietnam, Saudi Arabia). Their incentive is to avoid alignment while maximizing investment from both blocs. The "non-aligned" stance is economically optimal as long as the competition stays below conflict threshold.
