# Supply Chain and Industrial Strategy Theory

## HIGH-LEVEL

Globalization optimized for efficiency (just-in-time, lowest-cost sourcing). The 2020-2023 period exposed the fragility: pandemic shutdowns, shipping disruptions, semiconductor shortages, and geopolitical tensions revealed that efficiency and resilience are in tension. The response — friend-shoring, nearshoring, onshoring — is a structural shift that raises costs but reduces vulnerability.

The semiconductor supply chain is the most strategically important: concentrated in Taiwan (TSMC), dependent on a single Dutch company (ASML) for lithography equipment, and essential for everything from cars to AI to military systems. A Taiwan conflict would be the most economically disruptive geopolitical event in modern history.

## RIGOROUS

### Chokepoint Theory
Global supply chains have nodes where disruption causes cascading effects:

**Physical chokepoints**:
- Strait of Malacca (25% of global trade, 60% of maritime oil)
- Suez Canal (12% of global trade, 30% of container shipping)
- Strait of Hormuz (20-25% of global oil)
- Panama Canal (5% of global trade, water-level dependent)
- Bab el-Mandeb (Red Sea access, 15% of global trade)

**Technological chokepoints**:
- TSMC (>90% of advanced chips <7nm)
- ASML (sole maker of EUV lithography machines)
- NVIDIA (>80% of AI training GPUs)
- US chip design tools (Cadence, Synopsys)

**Financial chokepoints** (see sanctions.md):
- SWIFT, USD clearing, CHIPS/Fedwire

Disruption at any chokepoint triggers: supply shortage → price spikes in affected goods → inflation → monetary policy response → market volatility. The 2021-22 supply chain crisis demonstrated this transmission at scale.

### Just-in-Time vs. Just-in-Case
**JIT (pre-2020 paradigm)**:
- Minimize inventory, maximize efficiency
- Single-source suppliers chosen for cost
- Long, complex, optimized supply chains
- Vulnerability: single points of failure, no buffer stock

**JIC (emerging paradigm)**:
- Build inventory buffers ("safety stock")
- Dual-source or multi-source critical inputs
- Geographic diversification (reduce concentration risk)
- Cost: higher working capital, reduced margins, inflationary

The shift from JIT to JIC is structurally inflationary — firms hold more inventory (capital tied up), maintain redundant suppliers (less cost optimization), and accept geographic constraints (nearshoring is more expensive than offshoring).

### Friend-Shoring / Nearshoring Economics
Reorganizing supply chains along geopolitical alignment lines:

**Friend-shoring**: Sourcing from allied countries (US → Mexico, Vietnam, India instead of China). Reduces geopolitical risk but may not reduce concentration risk (Vietnam and Mexico become new single points of failure).

**Nearshoring**: Moving production closer to end markets. US-Mexico trade relationship deepened significantly post-2020.

**Onshoring/reshoring**: Bringing production back domestically. Most expensive option. Viable for strategic sectors (semiconductors: CHIPS Act; pharmaceuticals: BARDA) but not for mass consumer goods.

Economic costs:
- **Price increases**: Estimated 5-15% increase in goods costs from full US-China decoupling
- **Investment boom**: $200B+ in semiconductor fab construction (TSMC Arizona, Intel Ohio, Samsung Texas)
- **Labor competition**: Manufacturing reshoring into a tight labor market → wage pressure
- **Transition period**: 3-7 years to build new capacity; existing supply chains must function during transition

### Taiwan Semiconductor Risk
Taiwan produces >60% of global semiconductors and >90% of advanced chips. A Chinese invasion, blockade, or coercive reunification would:

**Immediate effects (days/weeks)**:
- Global chip supply halted for advanced applications
- Tech stock collapse (AAPL, NVDA, AMD, QCOM derive most value from TSMC-fabricated chips)
- US/allied sanctions on China → financial market disruption dwarfing Russia scenario
- Oil price spike (Strait of Malacca risk, military operations)
- Flight to safety: USD up, gold up, Treasuries rally

**Medium-term effects (months)**:
- Auto production halted globally (no ECU chips)
- Consumer electronics shortages
- Military equipment production bottleneck (guided munitions, communications)
- Global recession (estimated 5-10% GDP impact on advanced economies)

**Long-term effects (years)**:
- Accelerated fab construction outside Taiwan (already underway)
- Permanent restructuring of tech supply chains
- US-China full economic decoupling
- New Cold War economic blocs solidify

Probability assessment: most analysts estimate <10% probability of military conflict over Taiwan in any given year, but the tail risk is so catastrophic that it justifies significant hedging.

### Incentive Structure
- **Multinational firms**: Want supply chain diversification for risk management but resist it due to cost. The incentive is to diversify just enough to satisfy boards and regulators ("China plus one") while maintaining cost-optimized primary supply chains. Government subsidies (CHIPS Act, IRA) change the calculus by socializing the cost of reshoring.
- **China**: The "dual circulation" strategy aims to reduce export dependence while maintaining manufacturing competitiveness. The incentive is to keep foreign firms invested in China (market access as leverage) while building domestic capability in strategic sectors (semiconductor self-sufficiency as insurance against Western controls).
- **Vietnam/Mexico/India**: The primary beneficiaries of friend-shoring. Their incentive is to attract investment by offering competitive labor costs, regulatory accommodation, and geopolitical alignment with the US. The risk is becoming overly dependent on redirected supply chains that could shift again.
- **Governments (US/EU/Japan)**: Industrial policy is back. The incentive is to subsidize strategic sectors (chips, batteries, pharmaceuticals) while maintaining free trade rhetoric. The tension: subsidies for "us" while criticizing subsidies from "them" (China) undermines the rules-based trade system.
- **Workers**: In developed countries, reshoring creates manufacturing jobs but at higher costs passed to consumers. In developing countries (China, Southeast Asia), the restructuring disrupts established employment. The labor market impact depends on whether new jobs are in the same communities and skill levels as lost ones (usually not — a geographic and skills mismatch problem).
